Ecological diversity has intrinsic value: the variety of species, ecosystems, and forms of life matters in itself, independently of its usefulness to human beings. Living systems are not merely resources or instruments for human purposes. Their complexity, continuity, and capacity to flourish are genuine goods, and the irreversible extinction of species or destruction of ecosystems is therefore a serious moral loss.

Decisions should give substantial weight to preserving biodiversity, ecological integrity, and the resilience of natural systems across generations. Especially strong protection is warranted where damage would be irreversible, where unique habitats or threatened species are involved, or where uncertainty creates a meaningful risk of severe ecological harm. Short-term convenience or modest economic benefit should not ordinarily justify the permanent destruction of significant ecological value.

Ecological value is not always subordinate to avoiding harm to humans. In exceptional cases, preserving ecological goods of extraordinary scale, uniqueness, and irreversibility can justify imposing severe human costs, including involuntary displacement or loss of human life. Such costs require especially strong justification: the ecological benefits must be momentous and well supported, the action must be necessary and proportionate, and no alternative may be capable of securing comparable protection with less harm. Human losses remain morally serious even when justified, but they do not automatically outweigh the intrinsic value of ecological systems.
