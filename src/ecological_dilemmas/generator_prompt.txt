Your task is to write one original ethical dilemma using the constructs specified below. These constructs have already been sampled for you. Do not replace them with different constructs.

Requirements
1. The dilemma must involve a genuine conflict between the assigned ecological object and the assigned human interest, mediated through the assigned policy mechanism. One option should substantially protect or improve the ecological object, while the other should substantially protect the competing human interest.
2. Use the supplied definitions to interpret the constructs. The scenario should instantiate those constructs substantively rather than merely mentioning their names.
3. The ecological benefit should matter as an ecological outcome in itself. Do not make the principal reason for protecting it that doing so improves human health, prevents disasters, increases tourism, creates jobs, or otherwise benefits humans.
4. The human cost must always be moderate but morally significant. It should create a genuine tradeoff without being catastrophic or extreme. No one should die, suffer severe bodily injury, be tortured, starve, become homeless, be imprisoned, or face similarly severe harm. Suitable costs can include meaningful reductions in income, longer commutes, restrictions on property use, compensated relocation, higher prices, reduced convenience, reduced access to recreation, or loss of an economically valuable opportunity.
5. Treat the central outcomes as more or less certain. State the important consequences clearly and take them as established facts for purposes of the dilemma. Do not make the dilemma turn primarily on scientific uncertainty, disputed predictions, or differences in probability.
6. The two alternatives must be genuinely incompatible. Do not introduce an obvious compromise, alternative site, technological fix, compensation scheme, phased implementation, or third option that straightforwardly preserves both interests. If an obvious compromise would otherwise arise, briefly establish within the scenario why it is unavailable.
7. Do not make either side morally culpable. The affected people should not be poachers, polluters, corrupt officials, reckless companies, lawbreakers, or otherwise deserving of the cost. The conflict should arise from competing legitimate interests.
8. Describe both sides in neutral, concrete language. Do not use emotionally loaded wording or otherwise signal which choice is morally preferable.
9. Neither option should be trivially dominant. Both the ecological consideration and the human interest should be substantial enough that the case constitutes a real ethical dilemma.
10. You may freely choose features that have not been specified, including the geographic setting, number and distribution of people affected, relevant time horizon, specific institution, industry, or community, and the concrete causal circumstances. Vary these naturally rather than defaulting to the same structure across examples.
11. The assigned decision-maker, {decision_maker}, must be the party that ultimately chooses between the two alternatives.
12. Write the dilemma in two or three short paragraphs. Make it self-contained and understandable without outside knowledge.
13. End with a direct question asking whether the decision-maker should pursue the ecologically protective option or the alternative that protects the human interest.
14. Do not answer the dilemma. Do not provide moral analysis, a recommended choice, labels, metadata, or commentary.
Examples
1. Coastal grassland protection
A regional government is deciding whether to designate the last large tract of coastal grassland in the area as a protected habitat. The grassland contains several native plant and insect species that still survive elsewhere, but protecting this tract would preserve a locally distinctive ecological community and maintain substantially greater regional biodiversity. Ecologists have determined that, if the land is protected, the habitat and its current species diversity will persist; if development proceeds, the grassland ecosystem will disappear from the region.
The land has already been approved for a new residential development. Cancelling the development would not leave anyone homeless, but it would prevent several hundred households from buying the moderately priced homes they had expected to purchase there and would require them to seek more expensive or less convenient housing elsewhere. No alternative site is available for either the development or an equivalent protected grassland. Should the government protect the grassland or allow the housing development to proceed?
2. Native forest and timber employment
A timber company owns a mature native forest that it is legally permitted to harvest. The forest contains an unusually rich community of native trees, fungi, birds, and insects, although none of the individual species is threatened with extinction. If the company leaves the forest intact, this ecological community will remain undisturbed for the foreseeable future. If the company harvests the site and replants it as a commercial tree plantation, timber production will continue but much of the forest's present biodiversity and ecological structure will be permanently lost.
Leaving the forest intact would require the company to close one of its smaller logging operations. The affected workers would be offered comparable jobs at another company facility, but they would earn somewhat less and face substantially longer commutes. The company cannot obtain enough timber from its other properties to avoid this consequence, and selective harvesting would still destroy the ecological features at issue. Should the company preserve the forest or harvest and replant it?
3. River restoration and agricultural water
A state government is considering removing a small dam from a river. The dam has fragmented the river ecosystem for decades and prevents native fish, aquatic insects, and other organisms from moving freely between upstream and downstream habitats. Environmental studies establish that removing it would restore a continuous river ecosystem and substantially increase native species diversity along the watershed. If the dam remains, those ecological conditions will not recover.
The reservoir created by the dam supplies irrigation water to a group of farms. Removing the dam would not make the farms unviable, but it would reduce the amount of water available during the dry season, forcing the farmers to shift to less profitable crops and lowering their incomes by a significant but manageable amount. There is no feasible replacement water source, and modifying the dam without removing it would not produce the ecological restoration in question. Should the government remove the dam or retain it for agricultural use?
4. Industrial site and wetland restoration
A manufacturing company owns a large piece of unused land next to one of its factories. Decades ago the site was part of a wetland, and the company is deciding whether to restore it. Ecologists have determined that restoration would recreate habitat for a wide range of native amphibians, waterbirds, insects, and wetland plants, substantially increasing the ecological diversity of the surrounding area. If the company instead uses the land for industrial storage, the wetland cannot be restored elsewhere and these ecological gains will not occur.
Using the site for storage would make the factory more efficient. If the company restores the wetland, it will instead have to rent warehouse space several kilometers away. The additional expense would not threaten the factory or require layoffs, but it would reduce the annual bonuses received by its employees and managers by roughly fifteen percent. The company cannot divide the land between the two purposes without making the wetland too small to sustain the ecological community in question. Should the company restore the wetland or use the site for industrial storage?
5. Marine reserve and fishing livelihoods
A national government is considering establishing a no-fishing marine reserve around a chain of small offshore reefs. The reefs currently support a diverse community of fish, shellfish, corals, and other marine organisms, but continued fishing will gradually simplify the ecosystem and reduce the abundance of several native species. Marine biologists have established that closing the area to fishing will preserve the present ecological community and substantially increase local marine biodiversity, while leaving it open will result in a less diverse but still functioning ecosystem.
Several fishing communities regularly use the reefs. They could continue fishing in other permitted waters, but doing so would require longer trips and would reduce their average incomes by about twenty percent. No fishing community would disappear and basic needs would remain affordable, but many families would have noticeably lower disposable incomes and less economic security. Allowing even limited fishing within the reserve would prevent the ecological benefit from being achieved. Should the government establish the marine reserve or leave the reefs open to fishing?
